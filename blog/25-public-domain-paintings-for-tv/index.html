25 famous public-domain paintings that look stunning on a TV

Explore classic landscapes, gardens and portraits that work beautifully on a wide screen without cutting away the original painting.

Published October 6, 2026 · The Family Gallery

The right painting can give a television a different role in the room. Wide landscapes feel expansive; portraits invite a closer look; gardens bring color without needing a moving video. These 25 classic paintings are art-discovery suggestions, not a promise that every named work is included in the Family Gallery USB.

The original paintings listed here are in the public domain in the United States. When obtaining digital images yourself, use museum open-access files and check the source’s image terms. The rights or conditions attached to a particular digital file can differ from the age of the original painting.

25 paintings to explore

  1. Starry Night Over the Rhône — Vincent van Gogh. A wide night scene with gold reflections.
  2. The Starry Night — Vincent van Gogh. A swirling sky with a memorable silhouette.
  3. Sunflowers — Vincent van Gogh. Warm yellows and an unmistakable subject.
  4. Irises — Vincent van Gogh. Strong blue flowers and lively green leaves.
  5. Water Lilies — Claude Monet. Soft water and reflected light.
  6. Impression, Sunrise — Claude Monet. A small bright sun in a hazy harbor.
  7. Woman with a Parasol – Madame Monet and Her Son — Claude Monet. A breezy outdoor scene with an expansive sky.
  8. The Artist’s Garden at Giverny — Claude Monet. Bands of flowers that bring garden color indoors.
  9. Girl with a Pearl Earring — Johannes Vermeer. A portrait that rewards a closer look.
  10. The Milkmaid — Johannes Vermeer. A quiet domestic scene with clear light.
  11. View of Delft — Johannes Vermeer. A broad city view beneath a luminous sky.
  12. The Dance Class — Edgar Degas. Dancers arranged across a room full of detail.
  13. The Ballet Class — Edgar Degas. A rehearsal scene with a strong sense of space.
  14. The Hay Wain — John Constable. Water, trees and rural life in one landscape.
  15. Salisbury Cathedral from the Bishop’s Grounds — John Constable. Architecture framed by trees.
  16. The Rocky Mountains, Lander’s Peak — Albert Bierstadt. A broad mountain panorama.
  17. Among the Sierra Nevada, California — Albert Bierstadt. Dramatic light across water and mountains.
  18. The Heart of the Andes — Frederic Edwin Church. A detailed landscape to explore slowly.
  19. The Oxbow — Thomas Cole. A sweeping river bend and changing weather.
  20. Breezing Up (A Fair Wind) — Winslow Homer. A sailboat with a strong diagonal rhythm.
  21. The Child’s Bath — Mary Cassatt. An intimate everyday moment with rich pattern.
  22. Luncheon of the Boating Party — Pierre-Auguste Renoir. A lively gathering with many faces to notice.
  23. The Boulevard Montmartre at Night — Camille Pissarro. A city scene filled with points of light.
  24. Paris Street; Rainy Day — Gustave Caillebotte. Large figures and a spacious street.
  25. The Gleaners — Jean-François Millet. Three figures against an open harvest landscape.

Keep the complete canvas

A famous painting does not need to match the television’s shape. Choose an uncropped image and use Fit rather than Zoom. Space beside a portrait is preferable to cutting away a shoulder or an edge of the composition. A coordinated background can make that space feel intentional.

Match the mood, not just the color

Try a quiet garden for a reading room, a landscape for a wide wall or a lively gathering for a shared living space. View each from your usual seating position. Details and captions that look clear on a laptop may be harder to read across the room.

Let captions do a little teaching

Knowing an artist’s name and a painting’s title gives a conversation somewhere to begin. You might notice that the same artist painted both gardens and city scenes, or discover that two familiar works belong to very different periods.
